What is ToolBox?

ToolBox is a self-hosted personal management hub. All your data stays on your own server — nothing is shared with third parties. It covers four main areas:

Finance
Accounts, budgets, subscriptions
📈

Rich notes with categories

Read, highlight & annotate

Finance

Accounts

Track all your bank balances in one place.

  • Add accounts by selecting a bank, type (Current / Savings / Broker), and balance

FIRE Goal

Set your retirement target. Fill in your target yearly income, expected return rate, tax rate, yearly contribution, and current age. ToolBox calculates your required corpus, years to goal, and projected FIRE age automatically. The current corpus is pulled live from your bank balances.

Create named savings goals (e.g. "Holiday fund") with a target amount and optional deadline. Contribute to them incrementally and track progress with a bar.

Financial Glossary

Plain-English explanations for every financial term used in ToolBox.

Valuation

P/E Ratio Price-to-Earnings

Stock price divided by annual earnings per share (EPS). It tells you how many dollars investors pay for each $1 of profit the company makes.

Rule of thumb: <15 = cheap, 15–25 = fair, >25 = expensive — but always compare within the same sector. A tech growth company at P/E 40 can still be reasonable.

EPS Earnings Per Share

Net profit ÷ total shares outstanding. Shows how profitable a company is on a per-share basis. Growing EPS over time is a key sign of a healthy business.

P/B Ratio Price-to-Book

Stock price ÷ book value per share. Book value is what the company would be worth if it sold all assets and paid off all debt. P/B < 1 may signal undervaluation — or that the company has serious problems.

Market Cap Market Capitalisation

Total market value of all a company's shares: stock price × shares outstanding.

  • Small-cap: under $2 billion — higher risk, higher growth potential
  • Mid-cap: $2–10 billion
  • Large-cap: over $10 billion — more stable
  • Mega-cap: over $200 billion (Apple, Microsoft, etc.)
Intrinsic Value

The "true" estimated worth of a stock based on fundamentals (cash flows, growth, risk), not what the market currently prices it at. If intrinsic value > market price, the stock may be undervalued.

Margin of Safety

(Intrinsic Value − Current Price) ÷ Intrinsic Value × 100. A buffer to protect against estimation errors. Buying at a big margin of safety means even if your projections are slightly off, you still don't overpay.

Rule of thumb: >30% is considered conservative. Coined by Benjamin Graham (Warren Buffett's mentor).

DCF Discounted Cash Flow

A valuation method that estimates a company's value by projecting its future free cash flows and then "discounting" them back to today's money (because a dollar tomorrow is worth less than a dollar today). The discount rate used is usually the WACC.

Profitability

ROE Return on Equity

Net income ÷ shareholders' equity. Measures how efficiently a company uses the money shareholders have invested to generate profit.

Rule of thumb: >15% is generally good. Warren Buffett looks for companies with consistently high ROE.

ROA Return on Assets

Net income ÷ total assets. Shows how well a company uses all its assets (including debt-financed ones) to generate profit. Useful for comparing asset-heavy industries like manufacturing.

Profit Margin

Net profit ÷ revenue × 100. Shows what percentage of every dollar of sales ends up as actual profit. A 20% margin means the company keeps $0.20 for every $1 it earns.

Varies widely by industry. Software companies can exceed 30%; grocery stores might be under 3%.

FCF Free Cash Flow

Operating cash flow minus capital expenditures (money spent on buildings, equipment, etc.). Often called the "real" profit because it's the cash actually available to reward shareholders, pay debt, or reinvest — before any accounting adjustments.

Debt/Equity Ratio

Total debt ÷ shareholders' equity. Measures how much of the company is financed by debt vs owner money. High D/E = more leverage = amplified gains and losses.

Rule of thumb: <1 is conservative. Some stable industries (utilities, real estate) tolerate higher ratios.

Income

Dividend Yield

Annual dividend per share ÷ current stock price × 100. Shows the income return you earn just from holding the stock. A 4% yield means you earn $4/year per $100 invested, regardless of price movement.

Very high yields (>8%) can be a warning sign — the market may be pricing in a dividend cut.

Capital Gains

Profit from selling an investment for more than you paid. If you buy a stock at $50 and sell at $70, your capital gain is $20. Capital gains are usually taxable.

Unrealised P&L

Profit or loss on holdings you still own (not yet sold). It exists "on paper" and becomes realised only when you sell. Unrealised gains are not taxable until you sell.

DCF Inputs

WACC Weighted Average Cost of Capital

The blended rate a company is expected to pay across all its sources of financing (debt + equity), weighted by proportion. Used as the discount rate in DCF — it represents the minimum return the business must earn to satisfy all investors.

Typical range: 6–12% for most companies. Higher WACC → lower intrinsic value.

Terminal Growth Rate

The assumed annual growth rate of a company's cash flows after the explicit forecast period (typically 5–10 years), running forever. Usually set close to long-run GDP growth.

Rule of thumb: 2–3%. Never higher than the overall economy's long-run growth — a company can't grow faster than the economy forever.

Technical Analysis

SMA Simple Moving Average

The average closing price over the last N days, updated daily. Smooths out daily noise. Common periods: 50-day (short trend) and 200-day (long trend).

Signal: price above SMA = bullish momentum. "Golden cross" (50-day crossing above 200-day) = strong bullish signal.

EMA Exponential Moving Average

Like SMA but recent prices are weighted more heavily. Reacts faster to new price moves. Traders often prefer EMA when they want a more responsive indicator.

Bollinger Bands

Three lines: a 20-day SMA in the middle, and upper/lower bands set 2 standard deviations away. The bands widen when volatility is high and squeeze when it's low.

Signal: price near the upper band = potentially overbought. Price near lower band = potentially oversold. A "Bollinger squeeze" often precedes a big move.

RSI Relative Strength Index

A momentum oscillator on a 0–100 scale that compares the size of recent gains vs recent losses. Measures how fast and how much a price has moved.

Signal: >70 = overbought (possible pullback). <30 = oversold (possible bounce). Works best in sideways markets; in strong trends, RSI can stay extreme for a long time.

MACD Moving Average Convergence Divergence

Shows the relationship between two EMAs (typically 12-day and 26-day). The MACD line is their difference; the Signal line is a 9-day EMA of MACD. The histogram shows the gap between them.

Signal: MACD crossing above Signal line = bullish. Crossing below = bearish. Divergence between MACD and price can warn of trend reversals.

Performance & Risk

CAGR Compound Annual Growth Rate

The steady year-on-year rate at which an investment would have grown from its start to its end value, assuming compounding. Smooths out volatile yearly returns into a single representative number.

Example: $10,000 growing to $16,105 over 5 years = CAGR of 10% per year. The S&P 500's long-run CAGR is roughly 10% before inflation.

Sharpe Ratio

(Portfolio Return − Risk-free Rate) ÷ Standard Deviation of Returns. Measures how much return you earn per unit of risk taken. A higher Sharpe ratio means better risk-adjusted performance.

Rule of thumb: <1 = subpar, 1–2 = good, >2 = excellent. The risk-free rate is typically the current government bond yield (~4–5%).

Max Drawdown

The largest peak-to-trough decline in portfolio value during a period — your worst-case scenario if you bought at the top and sold at the bottom. Measures downside risk.

Example: if a $10,000 portfolio dropped to $6,000 before recovering, the max drawdown is −40%.

Alpha

The excess return of an investment compared to its benchmark (e.g. S&P 500). Alpha of +5% means the investment beat the market by 5 percentage points. Negative alpha means it underperformed.

Most actively managed funds consistently produce negative alpha after fees — one reason index funds are popular.

Beta

Measures a stock's price sensitivity relative to the overall market. Beta of 1.0 = moves in line with the market. Beta of 1.5 = 50% more volatile. Beta of 0.5 = half as volatile.

High-beta stocks amplify both gains and losses. Useful for managing portfolio risk — mix low-beta (defensive) and high-beta (growth) holdings.

Personal Finance

Savings Rate

(Income − Expenses) ÷ Income × 100. The single most powerful lever in personal finance — a higher savings rate means faster wealth accumulation and earlier financial independence.

Common targets: 20% = good, 30–40% = great, 50%+ = on track for early retirement. The FIRE community often targets 50–70%.

FIRE Financial Independence, Retire Early

A personal finance movement focused on extreme saving and investing to retire much earlier than the traditional retirement age. The goal is to accumulate a portfolio large enough that investment returns cover your living expenses indefinitely.

The "4% rule" is central to FIRE: if you withdraw 4% of your portfolio per year, history suggests it will last 30+ years. So if you need $40,000/year, your FIRE number is $1,000,000.

Corpus

The total investment portfolio required to sustain your retirement income indefinitely (or for a target number of years) without running out of money. Calculated as: Annual Expenses ÷ Safe Withdrawal Rate.

Example: need €30,000/year, safe withdrawal rate 4% → corpus = €750,000.

Net Worth

Total assets minus total liabilities. Your assets include cash, investments, property; liabilities include loans, mortgages, credit card debt. Tracking net worth over time is the clearest measure of financial progress.

Budget Adherence

How closely your actual spending matched your planned budget, expressed as a percentage. 100% means you spent exactly what you planned. Under 100% means you overspent in some categories; over 100% means you were under budget (good for expenses, only for expenses).
